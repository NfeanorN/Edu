Question 1 · 4 marks
Place Branding and its Five Guiding Principles: Analyze the principles of Distinctiveness, Authenticity, Memorability, Co-creation, and Place Making. Explain how a territorial brand must be a promise supported by "substance" and "symbolic actions" in order to avoid degenerating into mere propaganda.
Sample answer

Five principles for a place brand that doesn’t fall apart after the poster:

Distinctiveness — the place has to stand out somehow (genius loci, vocation, specific assets), not copy the same “smart green innovative” line as everyone else. Authenticity — stakeholders recognise the promise as real; if you invent an identity that isn’t there, people notice when they arrive (this is also why participatory branding matters in Kavaratzis). Memorability — stories, symbols, experiences stick in the head; if nobody remembers you, positioning failed. Co-creation — brand isn’t only a municipal office inventing a logo; residents, firms, university, visitors integrate resources and shape associations. Place making — the physical / spatial side: squares, retail streets, events, servicescape… the brand has to be visible in the place, not only online.

Brand = a promise of value. Substance = real projects (infra, safety, services, jobs, quality of life). Symbolic actions = logo, campaigns, events as communication. In the strategy matrix: Strategy + Substance − Symbolic → anonymous (nobody notices). Strategy − Substance + Symbolic → propaganda (promises without delivery). You need all three aligned. Papers also stress coherence between what you communicate and what really exists — otherwise image ≠ reality and trust dies.

Question 2 · 4 marks
The "Strategic Narrative" in Territorial Marketing: Discuss the importance of building a collective narrative of a place. How can different actors tell different stories that converge toward a single system of shared values and meanings, and what role does "primary" communication (real actions) play in this process?
Sample answer

Strategic narrative = the shared story that links past / present / future of the place (heritage + what we do now + where we want to go). Useful because place branding isn’t one slogan: lots of actors, lots of partial stories. Residents talk about everyday life, retailers about the centre, university about knowledge, tourists about experience… That’s fine — they don’t need identical scripts. They need to converge on shared values / ultimate purpose (wellbeing, vocation, survival of the place-system), otherwise the brand fragments.

How they converge: common strategy, workshops, positioning, mutuality (actors work toward a shared final purpose). Hankinson’s place brand as relationships idea fits here — consumers / users as co-producers, network as the vehicle for collaboration.

Primary communication (Kavaratzis model as used in the course): what the place “says” through real actions and the lived territory — buildings, behaviour, services, informal/spontaneous signals. Secondary = intentional marketing (ads, campaigns). The exam point is: primary / substance is the credible message. You can tell a nice story in secondary communication, but if primary actions don’t match, narrative becomes empty PR. So narrative is coordinated through strategy, but it only holds if real actions carry it.

Question 3 · 4 marks
Town Centre Management (TCM) as a Network Strategy: Illustrate the frameworks of TCM and Natural Shopping Centers (CCN). Analyze the evolutionary stages of these partnerships (from Infancy to Maturity) and the structural difficulties related to collaboration between small independent retailers and the public sector.
Sample answer

TCM = network / partnership approach to revitalise the town centre: local government + businesses (+ often property owners / citizens) work on cleaning, safety, events, marketing, accessibility… against competition from out-of-town malls and online retail (Caboni–Bruni type papers: centre as community that has to update its value proposition). CCN (Centro Commerciale Naturale / Natural Shopping Center) = treating the historic centre like a “natural mall”: many independent shops spontaneously organised in streets/neighbourhoods, managed as one experiential place-product, not one private shopping centre building.

Evolutionary stages of the partnership (roughly): Infancy — first meetings, weak trust, roles unclear, maybe a pilot event. Then a messy middle (growth / adolescence): more projects, but conflicts, unstable funding, free-riders appear (shops that enjoy cleaner streets / events without paying or joining). Maturity — shared vision, clearer governance, KPIs, more stable contribution mechanisms (sometimes BID-like fees), integrated marketing plan, TCM manager role more professional.

Structural difficulties: small independents fear losing autonomy (“why should I pay for collective marketing?”); little money vs suburban malls; distrust of the public sector / bureaucracy; change aversion; hard to coordinate many tiny shops; free-riders drain collective resources. Caboni–Bruni also stress digital (e/s/m-commerce) as both threat and opportunity — without network coordination, single shops struggle alone.
