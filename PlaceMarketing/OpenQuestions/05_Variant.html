Question 1 · 4 marks
The "Onlife" Challenge for Universities and Territory: Define the concept of "Onlife" as applied to educational institutions. Analyze how universities can act as territorial catalysts by integrating online experience with "onplace" experience, creating the need to physically experience the university environment and its social networks.
Sample answer

Onlife (Floridi, used by Bruni et al. for universities) = offline and online aren’t separate boxes anymore. Your choices and experiences get shaped by both at once — smartphone in the city, Zoom + campus, search + real life. For a university it means online isn’t just an “add-on” to face-to-face; it’s part of the normal value proposition, intertwined with onplace (physical campus, city, people).

After COVID, pure online unis and traditional ones looked more similar on screen. So the challenge for a place-based university: don’t compete only on cheaper distance learning. Use online to make people want the local experience — case studies with local firms, cultural heritage, language, managers invited into lectures, projects in the city. Online media should push recognition of the uni’s mission and create desire to visit / live the campus and its social networks when possible (that’s literally the MCQ point on Onplace universities in the New Normal).

As territorial catalyst: uni spreads knowledge, projects, human capital; links policymakers and stakeholders (meta-manager role in the paper). Place gives identity and differentiation; uni exports culture/knowledge and pulls talent in. Integration online + onplace = stronger place brand and a reason to choose this uni over a pure online one.

Question 2 · 4 marks
"Citizen-sourcing" and the Power of Citizens' Knowledge: Discuss the role of residents as "primary stakeholders." How can local government draw upon the collective talent and knowledge of the population to co-design the future of the place, overcoming the limits of traditional public management?
Sample answer

Primary stakeholders of a place = residents / citizens who live there every day (not tourists or rating agencies first). They deliver substance, carry the identity, and suffer or benefit from every strategy. If you ignore them, you get Belgrade-Waterfront-type conflict: projects without dialogue.

Citizen-sourcing = treating the population as a pool of knowledge and talent, not only as voters or “targets” of services. Living labs, workshops, participatory branding, open data / platforms, education that spreads the place vision… Government draws on what people know about streets, needs, culture — and co-designs with them. Same logic as high engagement in the tech–relationship matrix and A4A: actors integrate resources toward a shared final purpose (wellbeing / quality of life).

Traditional public management limits: vertical bureaucracy, top-down plans, communication as one-way announcements, slow reaction to complexity. Citizen-sourcing pushes toward more horizontal governance — ideas can come from many actors, not only the municipal office. Doesn’t cancel the government (still regulates, frames strategy), but shifts it toward meta-management / movie-director style: set direction, invite knowledge in, reduce change aversion by involvement.

Question 3 · 4 marks
The 4Ps of the Place Marketing Mix Reinterpreted: Analyze how the traditional levers (Product, Price, Place, Promotion) are adapted for places. In particular, examine the "Place Product" as a Value Proposition and "Price" as the sacrifice (monetary and non-monetary) borne by the user.
Sample answer

Classic 4Ps were built for goods. A place is a complex service system, so we reinterpret them:

Product → Place Value Proposition: not “one object” but a package of benefits and solutions the place-system offers to users (residents, tourists, firms…). Mix of tangible + intangible, co-created by many actors, always changing — streets, services, culture, networks, reputation. That’s why “defining the product” is hard in PM.

Price → sacrifice the user accepts to enjoy that proposition. Money yes (rent, taxes, tickets, cost of living), but also non-monetary: time, stress, distance from family, bureaucracy, overcrowding… Value ≈ benefits − sacrifices. If sacrifice is too high (or benefits fake), people leave or don’t come.

Place / Placement → spatial context where you can actually enjoy the value proposition (access, transport, distribution of services — not “SEO of the city website”). Promotion → more than ads: branding, PR, events, education/training, primary communication through real actions. Alone it becomes propaganda if substance is missing.

So 4Ps still help as a checklist, but place marketing needs partners, stakeholders and co-creation on top — otherwise you treat the city like a shelf product.
