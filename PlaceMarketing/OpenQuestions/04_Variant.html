Question 1 · 4 marks
Business Improvement Districts (BIDs) and the Problem of "Free Riders": Explain how BIDs work, focusing on the mandatory nature of participation fees. Analyze how this model addresses the problem of those who benefit from territorial improvements without contributing to the costs, and discuss the implications for horizontal governance.
Sample answer

BID = a defined zone (usually town centre) where businesses / property owners vote to set up a scheme and then pay an extra levy for shared services: cleaning, security, marketing, events… Basically the money that finances TCM-type projects without waiting only for the municipality’s budget.

The key trick is that after the vote passes, the fee becomes mandatory for the businesses in that district. Free-rider problem = shops that enjoy nicer streets / more visitors / collective promotion but refuse to pay or join the association — in voluntary CCN/TCM this drains collective resources. With a BID levy you can’t stay out once the district is approved: if you benefit from the place improvements, you contribute. That doesn’t magically create trust, but it stops the “I take the benefit, you pay” dynamic.

For governance this pushes toward horizontal arrangements: businesses manage pooled resources together (with rules), not only a vertical “municipality decides and pays / or shops beg for funds”. Government still sets the legal frame and often partners, but strategy and spending of the levy sit closer to the network of actors on the ground. So BID is both a funding fix and a shift from pure top-down to shared, horizontal governance of the centre.

Question 2 · 4 marks
The "A4A" (Actor-for-Actor) Approach in Territorial Systems: Examine the nature of A4A relationships, based on tolerance, mutual support, and systemic purposes. How does this vision overcome the transactional logic of traditional marketing in favor of the collective survival of the place-ecosystem?
Sample answer

A4A (actor-for-actor) — from the Polese et al. paper — is not just “A2A exchange”. Actors integrate resources and act with shared intentionality (orientation) toward a shared finality: the viability / survival of the system they belong to. In place terms: mutual support, tolerance, not opportunistic behaviour. You don’t only maximise your shop’s short deal; you also act for other actors and for the place-ecosystem, because your own benefit comes from the system staying alive.

Traditional / transactional marketing logic = firm sells, customer buys; or in early place marketing, government “sells” the territory as a product (demand-driven, more vertical). Roles are directional (B2C, promote → consume). A4A tries to drop that directionality: value is co-created, many-to-many, actors engage (behavioural + cognitive + emotional) and look beyond individualism.

Why this helps collective survival: under VSA / service ecosystems, the place survives turbulence if actors keep consonance and integrate resources for mutual value creation. A4A is the relational style that makes that possible — mutuality, shared purpose (wellbeing / quality of life / place vocation), less “I win you lose”. Without it you stay in transactional fights and free-riding; with it the ecosystem can adapt and stay viable.

Question 3 · 4 marks
Analysis of the Technology/Relationship Matrix: Describe the five quadrants of the matrix (from 'a' to 'e'). Focus in particular on Quadrant 'c' (Value Co-creation Driven) and explain why high technological investment, if not supported by strong relationships and stakeholder commitment, risks producing ineffective one-way communication.
Sample answer

Matrix = technology / knowledge / information diffusion on one axis, actors’ engagement / relationships on the other. Five positions (a→e) used in the course:

a — strong identification of stakeholders with the territorial project (high engagement side). b — medium tech + medium relationship → Demand-driven place marketing: government / managers promote the place toward a target, classic “sell what we have”. c — Value Co-creation Driven: high technology AND strong relationships — actors integrate resources, share knowledge fast, co-create the place value proposition (smart city tools used with people, not only on people). d — high relationship / democracy but weaker tech diffusion → lots of participation, but harder to evolve / adapt quickly to market challenges. e — low tech + low relationship → territory exposed to exploitation by unregulated external actions (weak local network + weak knowledge infrastructure).

Quadrant c is the interesting one for advanced place marketing (Bruni’s value co-creation driven vs demand-driven). Tech alone isn’t enough: if you invest in apps, sensors, portals, big data but relationships and commitment stay weak, communication becomes one-way — municipality broadcasts, nobody answers, no resource integration. Then you get expensive “smart” tools without co-creation, closer to empty promotion than to a viable place-ecosystem. High tech needs high engagement, otherwise you’re not really in c.
