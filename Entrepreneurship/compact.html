S1-Q1 · Elevator / Deck / Video / Summary / Plan / BMC

Basically these are different ways to present the same idea, just different length and format.

Elevator pitch — like 30–60 seconds talking. Problem + solution, no slides. You use it when you meet someone and have almost no time.

Pitch deck — slides for a real meeting with investors. More structure: market, team, money, ask…

Video pitch — same thing but recorded. Useful for online applications when you can’t meet face to face.

Executive summary — 1–2 pages text, short overview. If they ask “send me something short first”, this is it.

Business plan — the long one. Market, operations, risks, full numbers. For banks, grants, serious due diligence — not for a coffee chat.

BMC — one page with the building blocks (customers, value, costs, revenues…). Good to show how the business actually works / makes money.

So: quick talk → elevator/video. Meeting → deck or summary. Deep check → plan. How the model works → BMC.

S1-Q2 · TRL, BRL, IRL

Three readiness levels — they look at the project from different angles.

TRL (Technology Readiness Level) — how ready the tech is. Low = still idea/lab. High = it works in real life. Point: is the technology actually proven?

BRL (Business Readiness Level) — how ready the business is: customers, sales, team, how you make money. You can have great tech and still be weak here.

IRL (Investment Readiness Level) — ready for investors or not: numbers, traction, legal stuff, pitch, clear ask.

Purpose overall: see the gaps before you go to market or ask for money. High TRL doesn’t automatically mean high BRL or IRL.

S1-Q3 · Key sections of a pitch deck

Normal deck usually has: problem, solution, market, product, business model, competition, team, traction/finance, and the ask (how much you need and for what).

Sometimes also go-to-market — how you get customers.

Why it matters: investors see tons of decks. They want a fast picture — is the problem real, is the market big enough, can this team do it, how do you earn money, what do you want from them.

If you only talk about the product and skip market / model / ask, it looks incomplete. They’re investing in a business, not just a feature list.

S1-Q4 · Valuation formulas

Post-money = value after the investment comes in → Investment / Ownership %

Pre-money = value before that money → Post-money − Investment

Ownership % = Investment / Post-money

Exit proceeds = what you get when the company is sold → Exit value × Ownership %

These are basic because they decide how much of the company you give away and who gets what at exit. Same € amount can mean totally different ownership depending on valuation. Founders care about dilution, investors care about return.

5
S1-B1 · Exit €50m · 60% / 40%

Just use Exit proceeds = Exit value × Ownership %

Founder 60%: 50m × 0.6 = €30m

Investor 40%: 50m × 0.4 = €20m

30 + 20 = 50, so it adds up. (In real life there can be preferences, but for the exam this is enough.)

6
S1-B2 · €2.5m for 25%

Post-money = 2.5 / 0.25 = €10m → company value after the cash is in.

Pre-money = 10 − 2.5 = €7.5m → value right before the investment.

So investor gets 25% of the post-money company.

S2-Q1 · Tech transfer & carbon

Tech transfer here means moving know-how, experience and equipment between governments, companies, universities, NGOs — so climate solutions can spread.

Digital stuff that helps: AI (optimize energy/processes), IoT (sensors, monitoring), big data (see where emissions/waste are), smart platforms/grids. That can lower carbon intensity and improve efficiency.

How transfer helps: not everyone invents clean tech from zero. You take proven solutions, train people, install them → emissions go down faster.

8
S2-Q2 · FinTech & sustainability

FinTech = finance + technology (payments, digital banking, crowdfunding, also green finance tools…).

With tech transfer, FinTech can get better tools for data, monitoring, digital reporting, green loans/bonds. Then it’s easier to follow environmental rules and see what’s really green.

Impact on growth: cheaper access to money, more inclusion, funding for sustainable projects. So growth isn’t only “more GDP”, it can come with better control of environmental effects.

S3-Q1 · Business ecosystem roots & features

Business ecosystem = network of firms, startups, universities, investors (sometimes public actors) around a shared platform or value.

Roots: not only classic short buyer–seller deals. More like a living system — long relationships, actors depend on each other and create value together.

Main features: mutual dependence, different roles, shared resources/knowledge, co-creation, some common rules, and ideally win-win so the whole system stays alive.

10
S3-Q2 · Five arguments · ecosystems & startups

1) People share knowledge and tech — ideas move faster.

2) Startups can test ideas quicker with real partners/users.

3) Easier to find customers and partners.

4) Access to money, mentors, labs, infrastructure.

5) Innovation is expensive/risky — the network shares that cost.

That’s why ecosystems help innovation across industries and support startups better than working alone.

S4-Q1 · Mind Map · tools

Mind map = one main idea in the center, then branches. Good for generating ideas and seeing links fast, also in a team.

You usually write short words, maybe colors. Easy to add more branches.

Limits: can get messy, not great for deep financial analysis, and it’s not a business plan by itself — just a creativity/organization tool.

Digital tools: Miro, XMind, MindMeister, Coggle, Microsoft Whiteboard.

S4-Q2 · NABC model

NABC = Need, Approach, Benefits, Competition.

Idea is simple: explain an innovation clearly when you don’t have time for a full plan.

Need = what problem people have. Approach = how you solve it. Benefits = what they gain. Competition = why you’re better than alternatives.

Used in pitches, grants, R&D proposals, workshops — anywhere you need to “sell” the idea fast.
