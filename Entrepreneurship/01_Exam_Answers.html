S1 — Prof. Della Rosa · Pitching, readiness, valuation (50%)

This part is about how startups present themselves, how ready they are, and how money/ownership is calculated.

S1-Q1
Describe the differences between an Elevator Pitch, a Pitch Deck, a Video Pitch, an Executive Summary, a Business Plan, and a Business Model Canvas. In which situations would each be most appropriate?

These tools all explain a business idea, but they have different length, format, and purpose. In the exam, show that you know what each one is and when to use it.

1) Elevator pitch

A very short spoken presentation — usually about 30–60 seconds. You say: what problem people have, what you offer, who it is for, and why it is interesting. No slides needed.

Best when: networking events, chance meeting with an investor, first contact, “tell me quickly what you do”.

2) Pitch deck

A slide presentation (often 8–15 slides) used in meetings. It is visual and structured: problem, solution, market, team, numbers, ask.

Best when: investor meetings, demo days, accelerator interviews — when people expect slides and a short talk (5–15 minutes).

3) Video pitch

The same story as an elevator pitch or short deck, but recorded as a video. Viewers can watch anytime.

Best when: online applications, crowdfunding pages, remote contests, when you cannot meet face to face.

4) Executive summary

A short written document (about 1–2 pages). It summarizes the whole project in text: idea, market, model, team, money needed.

Best when: someone asks for a short PDF before reading a full plan, or as the first pages of a bigger document.

5) Business plan

A long detailed document: market analysis, operations, marketing, team, risks, full financial projections. It can be dozens of pages.

Best when: bank loans, public grants, serious due diligence, when partners need deep information — not for a first coffee chat.

6) Business Model Canvas (BMC)

A one-page framework with blocks like: customer segments, value proposition, channels, revenue streams, cost structure, key partners, etc. It shows how the business creates and captures value.

Best when: designing or redesigning the model, workshops with the team, explaining the logic of the business without writing a full plan.

Fast talk / hallway → Elevator pitch
Meeting with slides → Pitch deck
Online application → Video pitch
Short written overview → Executive summary
Deep check / bank / grant → Business plan
How the model works on one page → BMC
Exam tip: write a short definition for each tool + one clear situation. That is enough for a strong answer.
S1-Q2
What do the acronyms TRL, BRL, and IRL stand for? Explain the purpose of each framework.

These three “readiness levels” help people judge a project from different sides: technology, business, and investment. A startup can be strong in one and weak in another.

TRL — Technology Readiness Level

Shows how mature the technology is. Low TRL = idea, lab test, prototype. High TRL = product tested in real conditions and ready for use.

Purpose: reduce technical risk. Investors and partners ask: “Does this actually work outside the lab?”

BRL — Business Readiness Level

Shows how ready the business side is: clear customers, sales process, team roles, operations, pricing, revenue model.

Purpose: check if the company can create value in the market, not only invent tech. Many deep-tech projects fail because BRL stays low.

IRL — Investment Readiness Level

Shows how ready the startup is to receive investment: clean numbers, traction (users/sales), legal setup, pitch materials, realistic ask, understanding of dilution.

Purpose: tell founders and investors if the company is “fundable” now, or needs more preparation first.

Together, TRL + BRL + IRL give a map of gaps. Example: high TRL + low BRL + low IRL = cool tech, but not ready for market or money yet.

Memory: TRL = tech · BRL = business · IRL = investor-ready.
S1-Q3
What are the key sections of a pitch deck, and why are they important for potential investors?

A pitch deck is the investor’s first structured view of the company. Clear sections show that founders understand the business, not only the product idea.

Typical sections

  • Problem — what pain exists and for whom.
  • Solution — how your product/service fixes it.
  • Market — size and who will pay (opportunity).
  • Product — what exists today / roadmap.
  • Business model — how you make money (price, subscriptions, margins…).
  • Competition / differentiation — why customers choose you.
  • Go-to-market — how you acquire customers (optional but useful).
  • Team — why this team can win.
  • Traction & finance — proof: users, revenue, key metrics, forecasts.
  • The ask — how much money you need and what you will spend it on.

Why this matters for investors

Investors see many decks every week. They need a fast answer to: Is the problem real? Is the market big enough? Can this team execute? How do they make money? What do they want from me?

If sections are missing or fuzzy (especially market, model, team, ask), the pitch looks amateur and trust drops — even if the idea is good.

Common mistake: only talking about the product. Investors buy a business opportunity, not a feature list.
S1-Q4
Define pre-money valuation, post-money valuation, ownership percentage, and exit proceeds. Write the formulas. Why are they fundamental in startup finance?

These concepts decide how much the company is worth, how much of it the investor owns, and who gets what when the company is sold.

Post-money valuation

Company value after the new investment is added.

Post-money = Investment ÷ Ownership %

Example logic: if someone pays €2.5m for 25%, the whole company after the deal is valued at €10m.

Pre-money valuation

Company value before the new money arrives.

Pre-money = Post-money − Investment

Ownership percentage

Share of the company the investor receives for the cash.

Ownership % = Investment ÷ Post-money

Exit proceeds

Money each owner receives at exit (sale, acquisition, etc.).

Exit proceeds = Exit value × Ownership %

Why this is fundamental

  • Founders care about dilution — how much ownership they give away.
  • Investors care about return — ownership % × future exit value.
  • The same investment amount can mean very different ownership depending on valuation.
  • Without these formulas, negotiations about “€X for Y%” have no clear meaning.
S1-B1 · Bonus
A company is acquired for €50,000,000. Founder owns 60%, Investor owns 40%. How are proceeds distributed? Which formula?

Use Exit proceeds = Exit value × Ownership %.

Founder: 50,000,000 × 0.60 = €30,000,000
Investor: 50,000,000 × 0.40 = €20,000,000
Check: 30m + 20m = 50m

So the acquisition money is split by ownership shares. (In real deals there can be preferences/liquidation waterfalls, but for this exam use the simple formula.)

S1-B2 · Bonus
An investor offers €2.5 million for 25% equity. Calculate post-money and pre-money. What do they represent?
Post-money = 2,500,000 ÷ 0.25 = €10,000,000
Pre-money = 10,000,000 − 2,500,000 = €7,500,000

Post-money (€10m) = value of the company including the new €2.5m cash.

Pre-money (€7.5m) = value of the company just before that cash entered.

The investor owns 25% of the post-money company. The founders (and earlier owners) keep 75% after this round (before other complications).

S2 — Prof. Furlani · Technology transfer, climate, FinTech (25%)

This part links innovation/tech transfer with climate goals and digital finance.

S2-Q1
What digital technology can reduce carbon emission intensity while improving carbon emission efficiency? How can tech transfer contribute to reducing carbon emissions?

What is technology transfer here?

Technology transfer is a broad set of processes: the flow of know-how, experience, and equipment for climate mitigation and adaptation among governments, private firms, NGOs, research institutions, and other stakeholders.

Digital technologies that help

Examples often cited in this context:

  • AI / machine learning — optimize energy use, predict demand, improve industrial processes.
  • IoT (sensors + connected devices) — monitor buildings, transport, factories in real time.
  • Big data / analytics platforms — measure emissions, find waste, support better decisions.
  • Smart grids / digital platforms — integrate renewables, improve logistics and mobility.

These tools can lower carbon intensity (emissions per unit of output) and raise efficiency (same or better results with less energy and waste).

How tech transfer reduces emissions

Not every country or firm can invent clean solutions alone. Tech transfer lets them import and adapt proven technologies, train people, and install equipment faster. That accelerates mitigation (cut emissions) and adaptation (deal with climate impacts), instead of waiting years to reinvent the same tools.

In the answer, name 2–3 digital tools + explain that transfer = sharing know-how/equipment so clean solutions spread faster.
S2-Q2
How can tech transfer enhance FinTech to improve sustainability and help enforce environmental laws? What is the impact of FinTech on sustainable economic growth?

FinTech in simple words

FinTech = finance + technology. It includes digital payments, online banking, crowdfunding, mobile money, and also newer areas like crypto tools — plus green/sustainable finance platforms.

How tech transfer helps FinTech for sustainability

Through transfer of digital tools and methods, FinTech can better:

  • collect and analyze ESG / environmental data;
  • monitor projects and supply chains;
  • create digital reporting and compliance systems;
  • design green loans, green bonds, impact investment products.

That makes environmental rules easier to follow and harder to ignore — transparency goes up, greenwashing becomes more visible.

Impact on sustainable economic growth

FinTech can support growth that is more inclusive and cleaner:

  • cheaper and faster access to payments and credit;
  • more people and SMEs included in the financial system;
  • capital directed to sustainable projects;
  • better risk assessment using data.

So the point is not only “more GDP”, but growth with better environmental and social control.

S3 — Prof. Hrinchenko · Business ecosystems (12.5%)

Think network, not only one firm selling to one customer.

S3-Q1
What are the roots and the core features of a business ecosystem as a form of business interorganizational relations?

What it is

A business ecosystem is a network of organizations — startups, large companies, universities, investors, public bodies, sometimes users — connected around a shared platform, technology, or market opportunity.

Roots

It comes from moving beyond simple short-term “buyer–seller” transactions. Inspired by the idea of a living ecosystem: actors depend on each other, evolve together, and co-create value over time. Relations are longer, more complex, and more collaborative than a one-off contract.

Core features

  • Mutual dependence — success of one actor often depends on others.
  • Complementary roles — different players bring different resources (tech, customers, money, regulation).
  • Shared resources / platforms / knowledge
  • Co-creation of value — value is produced together, not only transferred in a sale.
  • Common rules or coordination — standards, APIs, governance, reputation.
  • Win-win (ideally) — the system stays healthy if cooperation works.
S3-Q2
Provide five arguments how business ecosystems promote innovations through industries and support start-ups

Write five clear arguments. You can use these:

  1. Knowledge and technology spillover — ideas move between firms, labs, and startups faster than in isolation.
  2. Faster experimentation — startups can test products with real partners and users inside the network.
  3. Market access — easier to find customers, distributors, and corporate partners.
  4. Support resources — money (VCs, corporate venture), mentors, incubators, labs, legal help, infrastructure.
  5. Shared risk and cost of innovation — R&D is expensive; ecosystems spread cost and uncertainty across many actors.

Optional extras if you need more: talent mobility, common standards, legitimacy/reputation from being part of a known ecosystem.

For the exam: number them 1–5 and give one short sentence each. Clear > fancy.
S4 — Prof. Chaikovska · Idea tools: Mind Map & NABC (12.5%)

Methods to generate and present innovative ideas.

S4-Q1
Analyze modern methods of generating innovative ideas by characterizing the Mind Map method — possibilities, application features, and limitations. What digital tools are used for creating mind maps?

What is a Mind Map?

A mind map is a visual diagram with one central topic in the middle and branches for related ideas, problems, options, and details. It helps you generate and organize thoughts without writing a linear essay first.

Possibilities (what it is good for)

  • Fast brainstorming alone or in a team
  • Seeing connections between ideas
  • Structuring a messy problem before a plan or pitch
  • Teaching / workshops / innovation sessions

Application features

  • Radial structure (center → branches → sub-branches)
  • Short keywords rather than long paragraphs
  • Can use colors, icons, images to group themes
  • Easy to expand step by step

Limitations

  • Can become chaotic if too many branches appear
  • Weak for deep financial or technical analysis by itself
  • Quality depends on facilitation and participants
  • It is a creativity/organization tool — not a full business plan or proof of market

Digital tools (name up to 5)

Miro, XMind, MindMeister, Coggle, Microsoft Whiteboard. (Also common: FigJam, Canva whiteboard, MindNode.)

S4-Q2
Explain the purpose, structure and areas of application of the NABC model

Purpose

NABC is a simple framework to present an innovation clearly and persuasively — especially when time is short and the audience needs a logical story.

Structure

N — Need: What important need or problem do customers/users have?

A — Approach: How do you solve it? What is your method, product, or technology?

B — Benefits: What concrete value/results do users get?

C — Competition: Why is your approach better than alternatives?

Areas of application

  • Startup and investor pitches
  • Grant and R&D proposals
  • Innovation workshops and idea selection
  • Internal presentations to managers or partners
  • Any situation where you must “sell” an idea without a full business plan

NABC forces you to connect need → solution → value → differentiation. That makes the pitch stronger than only describing the technology.

Memory hook: Need → Approach → Benefits → Competition.
