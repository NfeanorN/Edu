Game Theory

Source: Robert H. Frank, Microeconomics and Behavior, Chapter 12 — «A Game-Theoretic Approach to Strategic Behavior». All examples below are from the textbook.

1. What is a game?

A game has players, strategies (possible actions), and payoffs (outcomes). Your best move depends on what others do — this is strategic behaviour. Frank uses Ch. 12 before oligopoly (Ch. 13), where firms must guess rivals' reactions.

2. Prisoner's Dilemma — Table 12.1

Two suspects are questioned separately. Each chooses Confess or Don't confess. Payoffs are years in prison (lower number = better for the player).

Each player does better by confessing no matter what the other does — that is a dominant strategy. But when both confess, both are worse off than if both had stayed silent. The Nash equilibrium is (Confess, Confess), even though (Silent, Silent) is better for both.

3. Nash equilibrium

A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where no player wants to change their move alone, given what others chose. In the prisoner's dilemma, confessing is a Nash equilibrium because if the other confesses, you still prefer to confess; if the other is silent, you still prefer to confess.

4. Link to oligopoly (Ch. 13)

Two firms can keep prices high (like "don't confess") or undercut (like "confess"). Each firm gains from cheating, but if both cheat, industry profit falls — same structure as the dilemma. Frank's Cournot and Bertrand models (Ch. 13) show different ways firms compete after this strategic logic.

Dominant strategy = best choice regardless of rival. Nash = no one wants to deviate alone. Prisoner's dilemma = Nash is not the best joint outcome.
