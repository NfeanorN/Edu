1. Opportunity Cost — Should I work first or go to college first?

EXAMPLE 1.4 · Chapter 1, p. 6 Syllabus topic 14

The book says the cost of college is not only tuition, fees, books and housing. There is one more cost: the money you do not earn while you study. Economists call this the opportunity cost of your time.

Here is the key fact from the book: wages grow with experience. So the longer you work before college, the more money you must give up to study. The opportunity cost is therefore smallest right after high school, when your wage is still low.

On the benefit side, the book lists two gains. First, a degree raises your pay a lot, and the earlier you graduate, the more years you enjoy that higher pay. Second, jobs for educated people are usually more pleasant.

The forgone wage is the height of the purple curve. It is low at 20 and high at 50.

How to read this graph

  1. The horizontal axis is your age. The vertical axis is the salary you would earn if you did not study.
  2. The purple curve rises because pay grows with experience.
  3. The height of the curve at any age is the opportunity cost of one year at college at that age.
  4. At 20 the curve is low, so studying is cheap. At 50 the curve is high, so studying is expensive.
For most people it makes sense to go to college first and work afterwards. As the book puts it, "certainly it makes more sense to attend college at age 20 than at age 50."

Главная мысль: настоящая цена учёбы — это зарплата, которую вы не получили. Зарплата растёт с опытом, поэтому в 20 лет упущенный заработок маленький, а в 50 — большой. Отсюда вывод книги: учиться выгоднее сразу после школы.

2. Marginal vs Average — Should Tom launch another boat?

EXAMPLE 1.9 · Chapter 1, p. 10 Syllabus topic 4

Tom runs a small fishing fleet of three boats. His daily cost is $300, so the average cost is $100 per boat. His daily revenue from fish is $600, so the average revenue is $200 per boat.

Tom thinks: "My revenue per boat ($200) is bigger than my cost per boat ($100), so I should launch a fourth boat." Is this a sound decision?

The book's answer is no — the averages do not tell him what he needs to know. He must compare the marginal benefit of the next boat with its marginal cost of $100. The book gives the revenue numbers:

BoatsTotal revenueMarginal benefit of that boatMarginal costLaunch it?
1$300$300$100Yes
2$480$180$100Yes
3$600$120$100Yes — stop here
4$640$40$100No

Green bars stand above the $100 line, the red bar does not.

How to read this graph

  1. Each bar is the extra revenue that one more boat brings, not the average.
  2. The red dashed line is the marginal cost of a boat, $100 — the same for every boat.
  3. Launch a boat while its bar is taller than the line. Boats 1, 2 and 3 pass this test.
  4. The fourth bar ($40) is below the line, so the fourth boat loses $60 a day.
Tom should launch three boats, not four. The book's Self-Test 1.4 asks what happens if marginal cost is $150 instead: then only boats 1 and 2 pass, so he launches two.

Классическая ловушка: средняя выручка ($200 на лодку) ничего не говорит о том, сколько принесёт следующая лодка. Сравнивать надо предельную выгоду с предельными издержками. Четвёртая лодка добавляет всего $40 при затратах $100 — она убыточна.

3. MB = MC on a Graph — How long should Susan talk to Hal?

EXAMPLE 1.11 · Chapter 1, p. 12 · Figure 1.1 Syllabus topic 32

Susan pays 4 cents per minute to call her boyfriend Hal. There is no monthly fee, and part-minutes cost the same rate, so a 30-second call costs 2 cents. Curve MB shows how much one more minute of talking is worth to her.

How many minutes should she spend on the phone with Hal each month?

The MB curve slopes down. The book explains why: the more you have already talked, the less an extra minute is worth to you. The MC curve is flat at 4 cents, because every minute costs the same.

Figure 1.1: the best quantity is where MB crosses MC — 400 minutes a month.

How to read this graph

  1. The horizontal axis is minutes of talking per month. The vertical axis is cents.
  2. The purple MB line falls: minute number 500 is worth less to Susan than minute number 100.
  3. The red MC line is flat at 4 cents because the price per minute never changes.
  4. Left of the crossing, MB is above MC: one more minute is worth more than it costs, so she should talk longer.
  5. Right of the crossing, MB is below MC: she is paying more than the minute is worth, so she should talk less.
  6. Only at the crossing point is there nothing left to gain. That is the optimum.
400 minutes per month — the quantity where the two curves cross.

Правило «MB = MC» в чистом виде. Слева от пересечения ещё выгодно говорить дольше, справа — уже переплата. Та же логика позже даёт условие максимума прибыли MR = MC.

4. A Kinked Budget Line — Gigawatt Power's quantity discount

EXAMPLE 3.1 · Chapter 3, p. 57 · Figure 3.6 Syllabus topic 29

Gigawatt Power charges $0.10 per kWh for the first 1,000 kWh each month, and only $0.05 per kWh after that. The customer has $400 a month. On the vertical axis we put the "composite good" — all the money left for everything else.

Figure 3.6: the discount makes the budget line bend outward at 1,000 kWh.

How to read this graph

  1. Start at the top left: buy no electricity and you keep all $400.
  2. For the first 1,000 kWh each unit costs $0.10, so the line falls by $100 over that stretch. That is the steep part, slope −0.10. You arrive at the point (1,000 kWh, $300).
  3. After 1,000 kWh the price drops to $0.05, so each extra kWh costs you half as much of the other goods. The line becomes flatter, slope −0.05.
  4. Your remaining $300 at $0.05 buys 6,000 more kWh, so the line hits the bottom axis at 7,000 kWh.
  5. The bend is called the kink. It exists because the opportunity cost of electricity is not constant.
A quantity discount gives a budget constraint made of two straight pieces: steep first, flat after the kink.

Скидка за объём ломает бюджетную линию. До 1000 кВт·ч наклон −0,10, после — −0,05, излом в точке (1000; $300). Книга делает из этого забавный вывод: тот, кто уже потребляет больше 1000 кВт·ч, скорее оставит лампочку на крыльце включённой — для него каждый лишний кВт·ч вдвое дешевле.

5. Same Budget, Same Decision — Gowdy's fishing trip

EXAMPLE 3.2 · Chapter 3, p. 58 Syllabus topic 14

Gowdy plans a fishing trip. Petrol costs $4 a gallon and the round trip uses 5 gallons. Two different bad things happen to him on two different occasions:

  • Case A. He fills his 21-gallon tank the night before. In the morning a thief has siphoned out all but 1 gallon — he lost 20 gallons, worth $80.
  • Case B. He plans to buy petrol on the way out. In the morning he finds he has lost $80 from his wallet.
Should his decision about whether to take the trip differ in the two cases?

Many people feel the stolen petrol is different, because it feels tied to the trip. But look at the budget constraint. In both cases Gowdy is $80 poorer, and in both cases the trip still costs him 5 gallons × $4 = $20 of petrol from here on. The price of the trip has not changed; only his wealth has.

The line shifts inward in parallel. The slope — the price of petrol — is unchanged.

How to read this graph

  1. The grey dashed line is Gowdy's budget before anything goes wrong.
  2. Losing $80 moves the line inward, but the shift is parallel: prices did not change, only income did.
  3. A parallel shift is exactly what a pure loss of wealth looks like. Both cases produce the same new line.
  4. The book's rule: if the budget constraint is the same and tastes are the same, the rational choice must be the same.
His decision should be the same in both cases. Whatever he does after losing $80 in cash, he should also do after losing $80 of petrol.

Одинаковое бюджетное ограничение → одинаковое решение. В обоих случаях человек беднее на $80, а цена поездки осталась $20. Ощущение, что «украли именно бензин для поездки», — психологическая ловушка, а не экономический аргумент.

6. Consumer Surplus — What a petrol price rise costs you

EXAMPLE 5.1 · Chapter 5, p. 135 · Figure 5.5 Syllabus topic 35

One person's demand for petrol is P = 10 − Q, where P is dollars per gallon and Q is gallons per week. Her income is $1,000 a week. The price is $2, and an oil import restriction pushes it to $3.

By how much will her consumer surplus fall?
PriceShe buysConsumer surplus
$2/gal8 gal/wk½ × (10 − 2) × 8 = $32.00
$3/gal7 gal/wk½ × (10 − 3) × 7 = $24.50
Loss−1 gal/wk32 − 24.50 = $7.50/wk

Figure 5.5: the shaded band between the two price lines is the surplus she loses.

How to read this graph

  1. The demand line starts at $10 on the price axis. That $10 is the most she would pay for the very first gallon.
  2. At any quantity, the height of the demand curve is the most she would pay for one more gallon.
  3. Consumer surplus is the area between the demand curve and the price she actually pays — a triangle here.
  4. Raise the price line from $2 to $3 and the triangle shrinks. The band you lose is the shaded shape.
  5. Part of the loss is money she now pays on gallons she still buys; part is the pleasure of the gallon she stops buying.
Her consumer surplus falls from $32 to $24.50, a loss of $7.50 per week.

Излишек потребителя = площадь между кривой спроса и ценой. При $2 это ½·8·8 = $32, при $3 — ½·7·7 = $24,50. Потеря $7,50 в неделю — заштрихованная трапеция. Высота кривой спроса в любой точке — максимальная готовность платить за следующий галлон.

7. Intertemporal Choice — A higher interest rate makes you save

EXAMPLE 5.3 · Chapter 5, p. 149 · Figure 5.18 Syllabus topic 8

You have current income $100,000 and future income $154,000. You can borrow and lend at r = 0.1. At this rate you choose to consume exactly your income in each period — you neither save nor borrow. This point is called A, your endowment.

True or false: a rise in r to 0.4 will cause you to save some of your current income.
Interest rateHorizontal intercept (present value)Vertical intercept (future value)Slope
r = 0.1 (line B)100,000 + 154,000/1.1 = 240,000154,000 + 1.1×100,000 = 264,000−1.1
r = 0.4 (line B′)100,000 + 154,000/1.4 = 210,000154,000 + 1.4×100,000 = 294,000−1.4

Figure 5.18: the budget line rotates around the endowment point A, because A is always affordable.

How to read this graph

  1. The horizontal axis is consumption now, the vertical axis is consumption next period.
  2. The slope is always −(1 + r): give up $1 today and you get $(1 + r) tomorrow.
  3. Point A is your income in both periods. You can always just consume it, so every budget line must pass through A. That is why the line rotates instead of shifting.
  4. A higher r makes the line steeper: saving now buys more future consumption than before.
  5. At A the slope of your indifference curve (your marginal rate of time preference) is 1.1, but the new line has slope 1.4. The market now pays you more to wait than you demand, so you move up and to the left, to D.
True. At the new bundle D you consume less now and more in the future — that is, you save.

Линия межвременного бюджета всегда проходит через точку дохода A, поэтому при росте ставки она поворачивается вокруг A и становится круче (наклон −(1+r)). В точке A ваша норма временного предпочтения 1,1, а рынок теперь платит 1,4 — выгодно отложить потребление. Ответ: правда, вы начнёте сберегать.

8. From Production Function to Cost Curves

EXAMPLE 9.1 · Chapter 9, p. 269 · Figures 9.3–9.4 Syllabus topic 2 topic 5

The production function is Q = 3KL. Capital costs $2 per machine-hour, labour costs $24 per person-hour, and in the short run capital is fixed at K = 4.

Graph the TC, VC and FC curves for this process.

Follow the book's three steps:

  1. Put K = 4 into the production function: Q = 3 × 4 × L = 12L. Output is simply proportional to labour.
  2. Turn it around to find the labour needed for any output: L = Q/12.
  3. Add up what the inputs cost:
    TC = ($2 × 4) + ($24 × Q/12) = $8 + $2Q per hour.

So fixed cost is the $8 spent on capital, and variable cost is 2Q.

Figure 9.4: TC sits exactly $8 above VC at every output level.

How to read this graph

  1. FC is a flat line at $8. Fixed cost does not change when output changes — that is what "fixed" means.
  2. VC starts at the origin: produce nothing and you hire nobody, so variable cost is zero.
  3. VC is a straight line here, not a curve. That is unusual, and it happens because this production function has constant returns to labour — there is no region of diminishing returns.
  4. TC = FC + VC, so the TC line is the VC line lifted by $8. The vertical gap between them is always the fixed cost.
  5. The slope of both VC and TC is $2, which is the marginal cost per unit.
FC = $8/hr, VC = $2Q/hr, TC = $8 + $2Q per hour.

Как из производственной функции получить издержки: подставить фиксированный K, выразить L через Q, умножить на цены факторов. Здесь TC = 8 + 2Q: постоянные $8 — это капитал, переменные 2Q — труд. Прямая VC (а не изогнутая) означает постоянную отдачу от труда.

How to read this graph

  1. AFC (grey dashed) always falls. The same $30 is spread over more and more bags, so it never turns back up.
  2. The gap between ATC and AVC is AFC. The two curves get closer and closer as output grows, but never meet.

10. Economic Profit vs Accounting Profit

EXAMPLE 10.1 · Chapter 10, p. 301 · Figure 10.1 Syllabus topic 15

A firm makes 100 units a week using 10 units of capital and 10 units of labour. Each factor costs $10 a unit, and output sells for $2.50 a unit, so revenue is $250. The firm owns half of its capital and rents the other 5 units.

What are the firm's economic and accounting profit for the week?
Economic profitAccounting profit
Gross receipts$250$250
Direct labour cost($100)($100)
Direct capital cost (rented)($50)($50)
Implicit capital cost (owned)($50)—
Profit$50$100

Both bars are the same $250 of revenue. Only the accountant's bar leaves out the cost of the owner's own capital.

How to read this graph

  1. Each column is the same $250 of weekly revenue, sliced into costs and what is left over.
  2. Labour and rented capital are direct costs: real money leaves the firm. Both columns subtract them.
  3. The orange slice is the implicit cost of the capital the owner already owns. No money leaves the firm, so the accountant ignores it — but the economist does not, because that capital could have earned $50 somewhere else.
  4. That single slice is the whole difference: $100 accounting profit versus $50 economic profit.
  5. The $50 implicit cost is the firm's normal profit. Economic profit is whatever is earned on top of normal profit.
Accounting profit $100, economic profit $50. Note that buying versus renting the capital changes the accounting profit but leaves economic profit untouched.

Бухгалтер вычитает только реально уплаченные деньги, экономист — ещё и альтернативную стоимость собственного капитала ($50). Поэтому бухгалтерская прибыль $100, а экономическая $50. Нулевая экономическая прибыль в долгосрочном равновесии означает не разорение, а ровно нормальную доходность.

11. Industry Supply — adding up 200 identical firms

EXAMPLE 10.2 · Chapter 10, p. 309 · Figure 10.5 Syllabus topic 36

An industry has 200 firms, and each one has the supply curve P = 100 + 1,000Qi.

What is the industry supply curve?

You cannot add prices — you must add quantities at each price. So the book does it in three steps:

  1. Solve the firm's curve for quantity: Qi = −1/10 + P/1,000.
  2. Multiply by the number of firms: Q = 200Qi = −20 + P/5.
  3. Solve back for price: P = 100 + 5Q.

The general rule the book draws from this: if each of n firms has P = c + dQi, the industry has P = c + (d/n)Q.

Same intercept, 200× flatter: at P = 200 one firm offers 0.1 unit and the industry offers 20.

How to read this graph

  1. Both lines start at P = 100. Below that price nobody supplies anything, no matter how many firms there are.
  2. Adding firms is a horizontal sum: pick a price, read each firm's quantity, add them up. The dashed grey line at P = 200 shows one such price.
  3. The industry line is 200 times flatter because at every price the industry offers 200 times the quantity.
  4. Flatter means more elastic: the same small price rise now brings a much bigger increase in total quantity.
  5. This is exactly why long-run supply is flatter than short-run supply — in the long run the number of firms itself can grow.
P = 100 + 5Q. (The book's Self-Test 10.3: 30 firms with P = 20 + 90Qi give P = 20 + 3Q.)

Складывать надо количества при каждой цене, а не цены. Отсюда правило: n фирм с P = c + dQᵢ дают отраслевую кривую P = c + (d/n)Q. Точка пересечения с осью цен не меняется, а наклон падает в n раз — предложение становится эластичнее.

12. The Monopolist's Marginal Revenue Curve

EXAMPLE 11.1 · Chapter 11, p. 347 · Figure 11.8 Syllabus topic 23

Find the marginal revenue curve that goes with the demand curve P = 12 − 3Q.

The book's rule for any straight-line demand curve: same vertical intercept, twice the slope. So from P = a − bQ you get MR = a − 2bQ, and here MR = 12 − 6Q.

Why twice as steep? To sell one more unit the monopolist must cut the price — and the lower price applies to every unit, not only the new one. So the revenue from the extra unit is always less than its price.

Figure 11.8: MR shares the $12 intercept but hits zero at half the quantity.

How to read this graph

  1. Both lines start at $12. For the very first unit there is no earlier unit to discount, so MR equals the price.
  2. MR reaches zero at Q = 2, while demand reaches zero at Q = 4 — exactly half, always.
  3. Where MR is positive (left of Q = 2), selling one more unit still adds revenue. Demand is elastic there and a price cut raises total revenue.
  4. Where MR is negative (right of Q = 2), demand is inelastic and a price cut lowers total revenue.
  5. A monopolist therefore never chooses to produce on the inelastic half of its demand curve.
MR = 12 − 6Q. (Self-Test 11.2 in the book: P = 100 − 2Q gives MR = 100 − 4Q.)

Для линейного спроса MR имеет ту же точку на оси цен и вдвое больший наклон. Причина: чтобы продать ещё единицу, монополист снижает цену на все единицы сразу. MR = 0 ровно на середине спроса; правее спрос неэластичен и монополист туда не заходит.

13. Price Discrimination — one firm, two markets, two prices

EXAMPLE 11.3 · Chapter 11, p. 353 · Figure 11.14 Syllabus topic 33

A monopolist has marginal cost MC = Q and home demand P = 30 − Q. It can also sell abroad at a fixed world price of $12, however much it wants.

How much does it produce, how does it split output between the two markets, and what price does it charge at home?

The book solves it in four steps:

  1. Home marginal revenue: MRH = 30 − 2Q. Foreign marginal revenue is flat: MRF = 12, because the firm is small in the world market.
  2. Total output where ΣMR = MC. Since the foreign market can absorb anything at $12, this gives MC = 12, so Q = 12.
  3. Split so marginal revenue is equal in both markets: 30 − 2QH = 12 → QH = 9, and QF = 12 − 9 = 3.
  4. Home price from home demand: PH = 30 − 9 = $21.

Figure 11.14: ΣMR follows MR_H down to $12, then runs flat along the world price.

How to read this graph

  1. The blue demand line is the home market only. The dashed red MR_H is twice as steep, as always.
  2. The orange flat line at $12 is the foreign market. It is flat because the firm can sell any amount there without moving the world price.
  3. The combined marginal revenue curve follows MR_H while MR_H is above $12, and then becomes the flat $12 line. That kink is at Q = 9.
  4. Output stops where this combined curve meets MC, at Q* = 12. Read it off where MC = Q crosses the $12 line.
  5. Of those 12 units, 9 go home (where MR_H is still worth at least $12) and the last 3 go abroad.
  6. The home price is read off the demand curve, not the MR curve: at Q_H = 9 the demand curve is at $21.
Produce 12, sell 9 at home for $21 and 3 abroad at $12. Selling more at home would force the home price down and cut profit, because the extra home units would earn less than the $12 they earn abroad.

Правило дискриминации: выпуск там, где ΣMR = MC, а распределение — так, чтобы предельная выручка на каждом рынке была одинаковой. Здесь MR_F = 12 задаёт и общий выпуск (Q = 12), и порог для дома: 30 − 2Q_H = 12 → Q_H = 9. Цену дома читаем по кривой спроса, а не по MR: P_H = 21.

14. The Prisoner's Dilemma

Chapter 12, p. 374–375 · Table 12.1 Syllabus topic 6

Two prisoners, X and Y, are questioned in separate rooms. Each one can confess or stay silent. The payoffs are the prison sentences below — and here a smaller number is better.

Y stays silentY confesses
X stays silentX: 1 year
Y: 1 year		X: 20 years
Y: goes free
X confessesX: goes free
Y: 20 years		X: 5 years
Y: 5 years
← Nash equilibrium

How to read this table

  1. Take X's point of view and hold Y's choice fixed. If Y confesses, X gets 5 years by confessing versus 20 by staying silent — so confess.
  2. If Y stays silent, X goes free by confessing versus 1 year by staying silent — so confess again.
  3. Confessing is better for X no matter what Y does. That is what the book calls a dominant strategy.
  4. The payoffs are perfectly symmetric, so Y reasons the same way and also confesses.
  5. The result is the bottom-right cell: 5 years each. Nobody can do better by changing strategy alone, so it is a Nash equilibrium.
  6. Yet both would prefer the top-left cell — 1 year each. Individual logic leads to a worse group outcome.

The book then uses the same structure for real markets. Coffee growers as a group would earn more by planting less, but no single grower will cut back. Cigarette firms all advertise and each ends up with a profit of only 250, because advertising mostly moves customers between brands instead of growing the market.

Both confess and get 5 years each — worse for both than the 1 year each they could have had.

Доминирующая стратегия — сознаться, что бы ни сделал второй. Оба сознаются и получают по 5 лет, хотя молчание дало бы по 1 году. Равновесие Нэша не обязано быть выгодным для группы. Та же структура объясняет, почему кофейные фермеры не сокращают посадки, а сигаретные компании тратятся на рекламу, которая лишь перетягивает клиентов друг у друга.
