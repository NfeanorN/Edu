Externalities

Source: Frank, Ch. 16 — «Externalities, Property Rights, and the Coase Theorem». All examples from the textbook.

1. What is an externality?

An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on someone not involved in the transaction. The market price then does not reflect full social cost or benefit → too much of bad activities, too little of good ones.

2. Negative externality — example

A factory dumps smoke on neighbours. Private cost (to the firm) is less than social cost (private + harm to others). The firm produces more than is efficient — same logic as Frank's pollution and «taxing externalities» section (Ch. 16): policy can move quantity toward the social optimum.

3. Positive externality

Vaccination or education creates benefits for others. Private demand is too low compared to social benefit → subsidies can help (Frank discusses public responses to externalities).

4. Coase theorem (Ch. 16)

If property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, private parties can sometimes fix the externality themselves — e.g. neighbours pay the factory to filter smoke, or the factory compensates neighbours. Frank stresses transaction costs: in real life, bargaining often fails, so taxes or regulation may be needed.

5. Taxing externalities

Frank (Ch. 16, «Taxing Externalities»): a tax per unit of pollution raises private cost until it matches social cost — like a Pigouvian tax. Carbon tax example: make emitters pay for environmental harm so market quantity moves toward the efficient level.

Negative externality: market Q > efficient Q (MSC > MPC at market output).

Private optimum: MB = MPC. Social optimum: MB = MSC. Deadweight loss triangle between the two quantities.
